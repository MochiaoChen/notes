Have you ever heard before people talk about the economy?

Perhaps you've heard it mentioned on the news. It's talked about because it affects everyone: people who make up households, the businesses we shop at, and governments. 

But what exactly is an economy? To answer that, the first thing we need to think about is resources. Resources are things we can use to make what we need and want. They can include:
1. Land
2. Raw materials (like oil, water, and iron ore)
3. Machinery
4. People

Let's think about sneakers. Sneakers don't grow on trees. We need someone to have a design idea, then we need raw materials like rubber, cotton, plastic, and metal. Finally, we need people to put them together or to build machines that do.

But there's a problem: resources are limited. We can't have all of the things we want all of the time. We have to make decisions about how our limited resources are used. 

So, an economy can be thought of as a system where a group of people, businesses, or governments make decisions. They have to choose:
(a) What to make
(b) How to spend their time
(c) What to buy

There can be an economy for a town or community, for a country, or even the world.

 Let's think about Australia's economy. All the parts of our economy are linked, a bit like a jigsaw puzzle. A decision made in one part of the economy affects the other parts.

In reality, the economy is complex, so we sometimes simplify it into main parts or sectors:

1. Households
   That includes you and me. Have you ever bought something from a shop? Perhaps you've even had a part-time job. The household sector is made up of millions of people making choices about what they buy, who they buy from, how much they save, and where they work.

2. Businesses
   This sector includes all of the companies we work for and whose goods and services we buy. They decide what to sell, what prices to charge, and who to employ. Businesses pay wages to households so they can buy things from other businesses, helping money flow around the economy.

3. Government
   When households and businesses earn money, they pay a part of it to the government. This is called tax. The government then decides how to use this tax money to help Australians (for example, by paying for hospitals, libraries, parks, roads, and schools).

4. International Sector
   Our economy is also linked to economies in other countries. Not all of the things we need and want are produced in Australia, so we have to buy some things from overseas, like cars and computers. Conversely, we produce things that people in other countries want to buy, like iron ore to make steel, grain, or education-related services.

5. Banks
   Banks are a type of business that deals in money. Households and businesses use banks to save or borrow money.

When you save money in the bank, the bank pays you a little bit extra, known as interest. When you borrow money from the bank, they charge you interest, so you have to pay back a bit more than you borrowed. Many people borrow money for major purchases (like houses or cars) or to build a business. 

The amount of interest charged or received is given as a percentage of the amount borrowed or saved, called the interest rate. For example:
1. If you save $100 with a bank offering an interest rate of 3%, you'll get back $103 after a year.
2. If that same bank charges an interest rate of 5% to borrowers, someone borrowing $100 would need to pay back $105 after a year.

Interest rates can influence what people choose to do with their money, how much they save, borrow, or spend. When interest rates are high, it's good for savers because they earn more interest. When interest rates are low, it's good for borrowers because they pay less interest, making their loans cheaper.

But how do banks decide what interest rate to offer? That's where the final piece of our economy puzzle comes in. The interest rates banks offer are influenced by Australia's central bank, called the Reserve Bank of Australia (RBA). It's not like other banks; you can't borrow or save with it. Instead, it has a special job to help keep Australia's economy running smoothly. 

We'll find out how in the next video. When we put all of this together, we get our whole economy. All the sectors are connected, and a decision by one can affect the rest. You aren't just part of the economy; your decisions can influence it.

What Is Economics? The typical first-year student walks into his first economics class with very little idea of what economics is. Many people start this class with very little idea of what economics is. You might have heard things like, "Economics is the study of money," or "Economics is another word for accounting," or even, "Economics is hard; don't take that class." None of those are true.

The classic definition of economics is the study of the use of scarce resources that have alternative uses. Basically, people need resources to fulfill their desires. While these resources cannot be infinite, human desires can be. Therefore, people must make choices about how to use their scarce resources, and economists study those choices.

All economic questions fall into one of two categories:

1. Positive Economics
   Describes "what is." For example, the question "Why do people use money?" is a positive question. A general rule of thumb is that if your economic model has no value judgments, it is positive economics.
   
2. Normative Economics
   Argues for "what ought to be." The question "Should people use money?" is a normative question. If a model includes value judgments, it is normative, since telling someone what they ought to do requires judging what is best for them.

Economics is also divided into microeconomics and macroeconomics. Microeconomics studies the behavior of individual agents and markets, while macroeconomics studies the behavior of the entire economy.

Furthermore, economists have their own branch of statistics called econometrics, which specializes in analyzing economic data. Since economic data usually comes from the real world rather than controlled experiments, econometrics faces unique mathematical challenges.

The tools economists have developed to study human behavior have broad uses outside of what we traditionally consider economics. Economists study not only markets, but also topics like crime, war, the family, religion, culture, politics, law, and even genetics. That is why it is not unusual to see papers by psychologists, sociologists, criminologists, political scientists, anthropologists, biologists, neuroscientists, or legal scholars being co-authored by economists.
